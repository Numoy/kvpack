# Kestrel Station Operations Manual (Revision 7)

*This is a fictional document, written for the kvpack examples. None of it exists in any model's training data, which makes it a good test of whether a cartridge really learned the content.*

## 1. Overview

Kestrel Station is a subglacial research outpost built beneath the Vardø Ice Shelf, 212 kilometres inland from the coastal supply depot at Port Illeq. The station sits 340 metres below the ice surface inside a melt cavern that was first surveyed in 2031 by the glaciologist Dr. Imogen Taverner-Achebe. Construction began in March 2034 and the station opened for continuous occupation on 9 November 2036.

The station is operated by the Northern Cryosphere Consortium (NCC), a partnership of eleven universities. Its primary mission is the study of the subglacial lake known as Lake Morwen, a body of liquid water roughly 14 kilometres long that has been isolated from the atmosphere for an estimated 1.2 million years.

Kestrel Station supports a maximum crew of 26 people. During the winter season (May to August) the crew is reduced to a core team of 9, known informally as the "overwinterers".

## 2. Station layout

The station consists of six pressurised modules connected by a central corridor called the Spine. From north to south the modules are:

1. **Hearth** – galley, dining area and the station's only room with a window, which looks into the ice wall.
2. **Bunkline** – crew quarters with 26 sleeping pods arranged in two tiers.
3. **Loom** – the main laboratory, including the clean room used to handle lake water samples.
4. **Tern** – the medical bay, gym and the hyperbaric chamber.
5. **Anvil** – workshop, spare parts store and the drill control room.
6. **Lantern** – power plant, water reclamation and life support.

The Spine is 118 metres long. Emergency bulkhead doors are located at each module junction and can be sealed within 11 seconds. The bulkhead between Anvil and Lantern is painted orange and is the only bulkhead that must remain closed at all times except during transit.

Access to the surface is through the Chimney, a 340-metre vertical shaft with an elevator car called the Gondola. The Gondola carries at most 6 people or 900 kilograms of cargo and takes 7 minutes to travel the full height of the shaft. A secondary ladder shaft runs parallel to the Chimney and is only to be used in emergencies.

## 3. Power and life support

Kestrel Station is powered by a compact geothermal loop that draws heat from a volcanic vent beneath Lake Morwen. The loop delivers a steady 410 kilowatts. A bank of sodium-ion batteries in Lantern stores up to 2.8 megawatt-hours, enough to run the station at reduced load for roughly 60 hours if the geothermal loop fails.

The station's air is kept at 20.9 percent oxygen and a pressure of 101 kilopascals. Carbon dioxide scrubbers in Lantern are rated to keep CO2 below 0.4 percent for a crew of up to 30. If CO2 exceeds 0.8 percent anywhere on the station, a Level Amber alert is raised automatically.

Water is produced by melting ice from the cavern wall and is recycled through the reclamation unit, which recovers 94 percent of grey water. Each crew member is allocated 45 litres of water per day, of which 4 litres is drinking water.

The station's heating keeps all modules between 18 and 21 degrees Celsius, except the Loom clean room, which is kept at exactly 4 degrees Celsius to match the temperature of Lake Morwen.

## 4. Alert levels

Kestrel Station uses four alert levels:

- **Level Green** – normal operations.
- **Level Amber** – a system is outside its normal range but there is no immediate danger. Non-essential drilling stops. The duty engineer must acknowledge the alert within 5 minutes.
- **Level Red** – immediate danger to life or to the station. All crew report to their muster point. Gondola trips are suspended except for medical evacuation.
- **Level Black** – loss of pressure integrity or fire in Lantern. All crew move to Hearth, which is the designated safe haven, and the Anvil–Lantern bulkhead is sealed.

Muster points are: Hearth for crew assigned to Hearth, Bunkline and Loom; Tern for crew assigned to Tern and Anvil. Crew assigned to Lantern muster at the orange bulkhead.

Only the Station Lead or the duty engineer may downgrade an alert level. An alert cannot be downgraded from Red to Green directly; it must pass through Amber for at least 30 minutes.

## 5. Roles and responsibilities

- **Station Lead** – overall authority on the station. For the 2038–2039 season the Station Lead is Oskar Lindqvist-Mbeki.
- **Deputy Lead** – takes command if the Station Lead is incapacitated. Currently Priya Ramaswamy.
- **Duty Engineer** – a rotating role held for 12-hour shifts. The duty engineer carries the station's only master key, known as the "brass key", which opens every door including the orange bulkhead.
- **Station Physician** – responsible for the medical bay and hyperbaric chamber. Currently Dr. Tomasz Wierzbicki.
- **Lake Science Coordinator** – schedules all sampling of Lake Morwen. Currently Dr. Aiyana Redcloud.
- **Drill Master** – operates the hot-water drill that reaches the lake. Currently Fergus MacAulay.

## 6. Reaching Lake Morwen

Lake Morwen lies a further 90 metres beneath the station floor. Access is through a borehole drilled with hot water from Anvil. The drill heats water to 86 degrees Celsius and melts a hole 30 centimetres wide. A fresh borehole takes about 31 hours to drill and begins to refreeze after 40 hours, so each borehole provides a sampling window of roughly 9 hours.

Before any instrument enters the borehole it must pass the "three-wash protocol":

1. A rinse in 3 percent hydrogen peroxide for 10 minutes.
2. A rinse in filtered ethanol for 5 minutes.
3. Ultraviolet exposure for 20 minutes inside the Loom clean room.

No instrument may enter the lake unless the Lake Science Coordinator has signed the wash log. Violating the three-wash protocol is the only offence on Kestrel Station that results in immediate removal from the station.

Lake samples are stored at 4 degrees Celsius in the Loom and are shipped to the surface in sealed canisters called "reliquaries". Each reliquary holds six 250-millilitre sample bottles.

## 7. Daily schedule

- 06:30 – wake-up bell (a recording of a kestrel's call).
- 07:00 – breakfast in Hearth.
- 07:45 – morning briefing in Hearth, led by the Station Lead.
- 08:00 to 12:00 – work block one.
- 12:00 – lunch.
- 13:00 to 17:30 – work block two.
- 18:30 – dinner. On Fridays dinner is followed by "Cavern Night", a film screening projected onto the ice wall.
- 22:30 – quiet hours begin. The Spine lights dim to 20 percent.

Crew are required to take one full rest day every seven days. Rest days are scheduled by the Deputy Lead.

## 8. Supplies and the Port Illeq run

Supplies arrive by tracked vehicle from Port Illeq. The convoy, known as the Illeq Run, departs Port Illeq on the first Monday of every month between October and April and takes 3 days to cross the ice shelf. There are no supply runs between May and September because of winter storms.

Each Illeq Run brings approximately 14 tonnes of cargo. Priority cargo, in order, is: medical supplies, spare parts for the geothermal loop, food, science equipment, and personal mail.

The station keeps a minimum reserve of 120 days of food. If the reserve drops below 90 days, the Station Lead must request an emergency air drop from the NCC, which takes a minimum of 72 hours to arrange.

## 9. Medical

The medical bay in Tern has two beds and a hyperbaric chamber that can treat one patient at a time. Every crew member must complete a medical check with the Station Physician within 48 hours of arrival and then every 30 days.

Medical evacuation to the surface requires the Gondola and takes a minimum of 7 minutes to reach the surface plus approximately 4 hours by vehicle to the airstrip at Camp Skua. Helicopter evacuation from Camp Skua is only possible when wind speeds are below 55 kilometres per hour.

## 10. Traditions

New arrivals sign the "ice book", a leather-bound logbook kept in Hearth that dates back to the station's opening in 2036. The first entry was written by Dr. Imogen Taverner-Achebe.

When a borehole successfully reaches Lake Morwen, the whole crew gathers in Anvil and the Drill Master rings a small bronze bell known as "Old Gerda". The bell was a gift from the town of Port Illeq.

At midwinter (21 June) the overwinterers hold a dinner in which every dish must be made from food that has been at least a year in storage. The winner of the annual chili contest, judged at the midwinter dinner, has their name engraved on the station's ladle trophy.

## 11. Communications

Kestrel Station communicates with the surface through a fibre-optic cable running up the Chimney to a relay mast at the surface camp. The relay mast connects to satellites and provides 50 megabits per second of shared bandwidth. Personal video calls are limited to 30 minutes per crew member per week and must be booked through the communications officer.

If the fibre link fails, the station falls back to a very-low-frequency radio that can transmit short text messages through the ice. The VLF radio is tested every Sunday at 10:00.

## 12. Leaving the station

Crew depart on the Illeq Run's return leg. Before departure, each crew member must return their personal dosimeter, clear their sleeping pod, and have a final medical check. Departing crew traditionally write a final entry in the ice book and leave one personal item in the "cairn", a shelf in Hearth where objects from former crew are kept.
